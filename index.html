Welcome, Founder.

Nobody gets their idea right the first time. Not the two Harvard students who wired together a demo of software they hadn't finished, for a machine they hadn't bought, to convince a stranger in New Mexico to let them try. Not the founders who paid strangers $100 to upload dating videos to a platform that would eventually become YouTube, for something else entirely. And not you, yet: which is exactly why this workbook exists.

This is a field guide for turning a promising idea into original insight: a real, evidence-backed understanding of a problem that gives you an unfair advantage in solving it. It is adapted from a 10-week, cohort-based innovation program built for early-stage founders, compressed here into a self-guided, three-week sprint you can run alone, with a co-founder, or as part of a research team.

It will not tell you whether your idea is good. Nobody can tell you that yet, including you. What it will do is walk you through a disciplined, repeatable process for finding out: define your problem, map your business model, surface the beliefs you're building on, rank them by how much they could hurt you if you're wrong, and design the cheapest possible experiments to find out before you've spent a semester (or a seed round) finding out the hard way.

The most important skill for founders is creative problem solving. Most founders run out of resources, time and money, before they run out of creativity. The trick is picking the right problems to solve with the resources you've got.Program philosophy, Session 1

How This Workbook Is Built

Three weeks, six moves. Each week pairs a short burst of thinking with a worksheet you actually fill in, right here in the browser. The moves build on each other in order:

  1. Define your problem: clearly, narrowly, and without smuggling in your solution.
  2. Map your business model onto the canvas that matches what you're actually building.
  3. Translate every entry on that canvas into an assumption, and separate what you know from what you merely believe.
  4. Rank, classify, and prioritize those assumptions by risk and by how quickly you could test them.
  5. Design a cheap, fast, falsifiable experiment for each of your top five assumptions.
  6. Run the experiments, read the evidence honestly, and decide your next move, including, if the evidence says so, a pivot.

Every chapter carries a Field Notes box, a short set of reflection prompts. These aren't busywork. They're where the actual thinking happens; the canvases just hold the output. Answer them honestly, in your own words, even when the honest answer is "I don't know yet." Your answers save automatically in this browser as you go.

A note on honesty

You are not being graded on conviction.

This process rewards people who can prove themselves wrong quickly, not people who can defend an idea forever. Treat every assumption you write down as a suspect, not a client. If the evidence doesn't support it, that is progress, not failure.

Four Ideas Worth Building On

Before you open a single canvas, it helps to understand the reasoning underneath this whole process. These four ideas show up again and again across the next three weeks, treat them as your operating system.

1. Time is money, so pick your problem carefully

The longer it takes you to identify and solve a meaningful problem, the more resources (grants, investment, or your own runway) it will take to stay focused on the work. Without a meaningful problem, you'll need a day job sooner than you'd like. That's not a threat, it's arithmetic. It's precisely why this program allocates the majority of its time to problem and solution validation, rather than building.

Worked example

The Goldilocks Zone

Plot potential markets on two axes: how large the market is, and how acute (painful) the problem is. The tempting answer, a huge market with a screamingly painful problem, is almost never available to a small team; if it were, someone with more resources would already own it.

The sweet spot for a founder with limited resources is a small but committed group of people with a problem painful enough that they'd pay to solve it today. Microsoft's first product was software for the Altair 8800, one of the first personal computers, and a niche so small that Bill Gates and Paul Allen didn't even own the machine when they wrote to its manufacturer offering a demo. They built the prototype on borrowed university computers in the two weeks before the meeting. The niche was tiny. The problem was acute. That combination is what let a two-person team compete at all.

2. Problem before solution, always

Every venture (a tech startup, a social enterprise, a research spin-out) needs to solve a real problem for real people before anything else matters. This program (and this workbook) is built around a simple roadmap for how ventures actually mature:

  • Founder-Startup Fit: do you have the skills, network, and staying power for this specific problem?
  • Problem Fit: does this problem exist, and does it matter to the people who have it?
  • Problem-Solution Fit: does your proposed solution actually address that problem? (More on this, it's a spectrum and not a milestone, in Week 1.)
  • Business Model Fit: can you deliver that solution to people sustainably?
  • Product-Market Fit: have you made something people want, that you can find and keep customers for, and that you can profitably deliver at scale?
  • Growth.

Notice where the arrow starts: identifying a compelling problem comes before anything else. Only after that should you develop a solution. If you find yourself back at problem-fit after weeks of solution work, that's not a step backward, it's the process working.

3. Desirability, Feasibility, Viability, and Responsibility

Design thinking spent decades asking one question: do people want this? That's desirability, and it works, right up until you realize that wanting something and it making business sense are different things. Everyone would like more legroom on a flight; nobody wants to pay for the four seats that legroom would displace. So teams learned to also ask: can we actually build this (feasibility), and does it make business sense (viability)? Balancing all three is the foundation of business design, and it's also a map of exactly why most new ventures fail: desirability failure (nobody wants it), feasibility failure (it can't be built, or not affordably), or viability failure (it can't be produced cheaply enough to sell profitably).

This workbook adds a fourth lens on purpose: responsibility. Just because something can be built, and people might buy it, doesn't mean it should exist. As a founder or researcher, you carry some responsibility for aligning what you build with social and ethical standards, not as an afterthought but as a design constraint from day one.

4. Nobody gets it right the first time

Across a large sample of successful startups studied by researchers, the substantial majority made at least one significant pivot, and many pivoted more than once. A pivot doesn't mean your original idea was bad. It means you listened to the market, tested, and adapted: the only strategy that has ever reliably worked.

The Math of Pivoting

Number of pivots = Total funding ÷ pivot burn rate

If a typical early funding round buys you 12–18 months of runway, and each major pivot burns 3–6 months of execution time to re-validate, you get roughly three shots before the money runs out. Every month you delay recognizing that a pivot is needed is runway spent defending the wrong thesis. Pivot early, the data is unambiguous that it's cheaper.

Refusing to pivot when the evidence says otherwise is one of the fastest ways to kill a venture. This workbook is built to make that evidence impossible to ignore.

Ground Rules for the Next Three Weeks

Borrowed from the cohort program this workbook is adapted from, six commitments worth making to yourself before you begin, especially if you're doing this without teammates or classmates to keep you honest.

  • Embrace all perspectives. If you interview people, or work with others, treat different backgrounds and different takes as data, not noise.
  • Be prepared to pivot. Your solution might look very different in three weeks. That's a sign the process is working, not that you failed. You can always return to your original idea; just follow the evidence in the meantime.
  • Progress over perfection. At this stage, speed of learning beats polish. Ship rough experiments, gather signal quickly, and iterate rather than over-investing in something unvalidated.
  • Share learnings, not just outcomes. If you're doing this with peers, a failed experiment that's clearly documented is worth more to the group than a vague success story.
  • Respect confidentiality. If you're working alongside other founders, what's shared in the room stays in the room. Ideas alone aren't especially valuable (execution is), but people still deserve to keep their ideas their own.
  • This is a shared experience. If you're facilitating this for a cohort, sometimes group progress has to take priority over any one person's pace. Plan accordingly.

Move 1

Define Your Problem

Academic innovators, more than almost anyone, tend to merge problem and solution. You've spent years getting good at building things: a model, a method, a prototype, a paper. That instinct to jump to a solution is exactly what this exercise disciplines.

What Counts As a Problem

A problem is a meaningful difficulty, friction, or unmet need that a specific user experiences when trying to achieve a goal in a particular context.

This is a narrower definition than you might expect. In a business or research-translation sense, a problem is not a general issue or an idea space, it has four required components.

  • It belongs to someone identifiable, not "people in general."
  • It exists in relation to a desired outcome, a goal the person is trying to reach.
  • Something prevents them from achieving that goal easily or effectively.
  • It occurs in a specific situation, not everywhere, all the time, for everyone.

The Problem Statement Formula

Describe your problem using only these four elements: who experiences it, when it occurs, what happens, and why it matters, and nothing else. It is forbidden to mention your solution, or any technological term tied to it.

Build it live

"When [specific actor] is in [specific situation], they struggle with [specific difficulty], which leads to [negative consequence]."

A strong problem statement is observable, user-anchored, goal-linked, and solution-agnostic. Fill in the four blanks below, they'll compose into your statement automatically.

Who experiences it?
When does it occur?
What happens?
Why does it matter?

Why Bother? Problem Validation Determines Two Things

First, whether there is a market for your idea at all. You might have a genuinely bright idea, but without a market, you can't build a sustainable business or research spin-out around it. Problems that are acute, genuinely painful, are the ones people will pay to have solved. This is the same Goldilocks logic from the introduction: a small, committed, high-pain audience beats a large, indifferent one.

Second, problem validation sets the scope of your solution. Charles Kettering, former head of research at General Motors, put it well: a problem well-stated is a problem half-solved. A problem with no boundaries has an infinite number of possible solutions, you'll build forever and please no one. Good problems are bounded: they require few features to solve well.

Historical case

Bounded problems win

In 1975, two college students noticed a narrow, specific problem: early personal computers were hard to program, which made them unattractive to almost everyone except hobbyists. They didn't try to solve computing. They wrote one piece of software, for one machine, for one small audience, and that bounded, specific problem became the foundation of Microsoft.

Do this now

  1. Draft your problem statement using the formula above (10 minutes). Say it out loud, does it name a real, specific person? Or does it describe "everyone"?
  2. Cross-check it (5–10 minutes). Hand it to a labmate, classmate, or even a stranger, and ask: is this clearly a problem, or is it still partly a solution pitch? Is it observable? Is it specific enough to test?
  3. Revise until your statement survives the cross-check without you needing to explain or defend it.

Move 2

Map Your Business Model

With your problem statement in hand, it's time to put the whole picture on one page: problem, solution, customers, and economics together. Pick the canvas that matches what you're building. All three work the same way: read them as a living document, not a form you complete once.

A Worked Example: Mapping IKEA

Before you fill in your own, it helps to see the reasoning behind each box, using a business everyone already understands.

  • Problem: Furniture is expensive. Less obviously, urban transportation constraints mean furniture has to fit in small cars and up narrow stairwells.
  • Customer Segments: Budget-conscious, mobile, often first-time furniture buyers, students, young renters, new households.
  • Unique Value Proposition: Frame it as We are building [X] for [Y] because [Z], this should shape your solution, not the other way around.
  • Solution: Flat-packed, self-assembled, self-service warehouse retail. Notice this box is smaller than the Problem box on purpose.
  • Unfair Advantage: Vertically integrated flat-pack design and self-service logistics that keep costs, and prices, structurally lower than competitors.
Why value comes before solution

Value isn't universal.

What you find valuable about your own idea isn't necessarily what someone else will perceive as valuable. Ask three different people to "sell" you the same ordinary object and you'll get three different pitches, the ones that land are the ones that meet the buyer in their own context. Keep this in mind as you write your Unique Value Proposition: you are guessing, for now, which is exactly why Week 2 exists.

Three Lenses of Canvas Risk

Beyond being a map of your venture, your canvas doubles as a risk-finding tool. As you fill it in, notice which boxes you filled in confidently and which you're guessing at:

  • Product Risk: am I building the right thing? (Problem, Solution, Unique Value Proposition)
  • Customer Risk: does the customer I'm picturing exist, and do they care? (Customer Segments, Early Adopters, Channels)
  • Market / Economic Risk: can this support a real venture? (Cost Structure, Revenue Streams, Key Metrics)

Problem Red Flags

Before you consider your canvas finished, stress-test the Problem box against six patterns that quietly kill otherwise well-built ventures.

Red Flag 1

The Reasoned Problem

A problem invented by the founder, not sourced from real users. Ask yourself: who explicitly told me they have this problem?

Red Flag 2

The Non-Problem

Something founders label a problem even though the people living it don't see it that way. Ask yourself: who told me this is actually a problem for them?

Red Flag 3

The No-Owner Problem

A vague issue with no clear person or group responsible for caring, or paying. Ask yourself: who cares enough about this to act, or to pay?

Red Flag 4

The Umbrella Problem

A problem defined too broadly, "climate change," "mental health," "education." Ask yourself: what exact sub-problem do we solve directly?

Red Flag 5

The Overview / Info Problem

Customers say they're overwhelmed or just need more information. Ask yourself: what have people actually tried to fix or find, before I showed up?

Red Flag 6

Your Goal as a Problem

Treating your organization's objective as if it were a customer's pain. Ask yourself: do I understand what outcome the customer wants, independent of what I want from them?

The Problem-Solution Fit Spectrum

Problem-solution fit isn't a single milestone, it's a spectrum of evidence. Think of each level as a bucket that fills with evidence over time.

Level 1 · Reasoned Fit

It's logically correct, in your head.

Example: hating flat bicycle tires enough to imagine a solid rubber tire that never goes flat, with zero evidence beyond "this should logically work."

Level 2 · Perceived Fit

People believe it would work, once they see it.

Example: finding that solid-tire product online and believing, as a buyer, it would solve your problem, even before you've bought it.

Level 3 · Demonstrated Fit

You can prove it works, under controlled conditions.

Example: actually running the solid tire over nails and glass to show, live, that it doesn't go flat.

Level 4 · Observed Fit

People use it, unprompted, in everyday life.

Example: seeing bike-share fleets running run-flat tires for years with no intervention from the manufacturer.

Move 3

Translate Your Canvas Into Assumptions

Every business model is built on a set of underlying beliefs, whether you're aware of them or not. This chapter surfaces what's sitting beneath the surface of the canvas you just filled in.

An assumption is a hypothesis about customers, problems, solutions, behaviors, markets, or economics that has not yet been validated with evidence.

If you can't point to real evidence (interviews, behavioral data, market data, a working prototype) for an entry on your canvas, it's an assumption, not a fact.

Six Kinds of Assumption

  • Solution assumptions: e.g., "Users prefer a mobile app over a web platform."
  • Customer assumptions: e.g., "Early adopters are biotech startups, not pharma corporations."
  • Problem assumptions: e.g., "Small clinics lose revenue due to inefficient patient scheduling."
  • Behavior assumptions: e.g., "Researchers will trust AI-generated summaries."
  • Market assumptions: beliefs about the size, growth, or structure of your total addressable market.
  • Economic assumptions: beliefs about pricing, cost structure, or unit economics.

You don't need hard evidence for every single decision: some things you already know to be true. But watch the gap between a general fact and your specific claim, that's one of the most common ways founders fool themselves.

Why This Matters

  1. It prevents self-deception. Early-stage work is a lot like a criminal investigation, you're operating under extreme uncertainty, and your job is to follow the evidence, not defend a theory you've already fallen for.
  2. It reduces risk efficiently. Not all assumptions are equal. Naming them lets you focus on the highest-risk ones first, the same way a detective runs down the strongest lead first.
  3. It turns activity into structured learning. With this structure, a pivot becomes a rational business decision instead of an emotional defeat.
The pivot math, revisited

79%

In studies of startups that found real success, the substantial majority made at least one significant pivot, and it was common for that to happen more than once. As covered earlier, your runway only affords about three pivots, so the earlier you catch a bad assumption, the more shots you preserve.

Move 4

Rank, Classify & Prioritize

Not all assumptions are equally important, and not all are equally uncertain. This gives you a repeatable system for figuring out which of your assumptions deserve your limited time in Week 3.

Step 1 & 2 · Plot & Score

  • High confidence, high importance: you've cracked the code here.
  • High importance, high uncertainty: you're in trouble if you don't figure this out. Critical assumptions live here.
  • Low importance, either level of certainty: not a priority right now.
Uncertainty scale (1 = very low, 5 = very high)

1: Real-world usage or market data strongly confirms it.
2: Behavioral tests or early prototypes support it.
3: Multiple customer interviews point the same direction.
4: Limited anecdotal evidence, or a few informal chats.
5: Based only on opinion or speculation.

Importance scale (1 = very low, 5 = very high)

1: If wrong, little impact on the venture.
2: If wrong, a minor, easily-adapted problem.
3: If wrong, meaningful changes needed.
4: If wrong, meaningful, possibly fundamental, changes.
5: If wrong, the venture would likely fail entirely.

Risk = Importance × Uncertainty

A quick way to surface which assumptions are quietly generating the most danger for your venture. The number itself is more useful than the label, because it lets you rank, not just categorize.

Worked Example: QuickPrep

QuickPrep is a fictional AI exam-prep app for university students, priced at €8/month. Here's how four of its assumptions score:

  • "Students want quiz-based self-testing." Low uncertainty (30 interviews support it) but high importance, a foundational assumption.
  • "Students will use daily quizzes all semester." High uncertainty and high importance, this is the critical assumption.
  • "Students will pay €8/month." High uncertainty but comparatively low importance, flexible, not existential.
  • "The AI can generate quiz questions from slides." Low uncertainty and low importance, a technical problem, not a business risk.

Step 4 · Classify the Type of Risk

  • Problem Risk: the problem is real and painful.
  • Solution Risk: your solution actually solves it.
  • Customer Risk: the target customer exists, and cares.
  • Behavior Risk: customers will actually change their behavior to adopt the solution, often the hardest to retire.
  • Economic Risk: the venture can generate sustainable revenue.
  • Market Risk: the total market is large enough to support a viable venture.
A useful mental model

Dependency logic

Read this chain top to bottom: a market must exist → customers must have a real problem → your solution must solve it → customers must adopt it → customers must pay → the economics must work. If an early link fails, everything above it collapses.

Do this now, classify your top assumptions

For each of your most important assumptions, fill out a risk profile card. Be concrete with validation criteria, "people seemed interested" is not one; "5 of 8 interviewed lab managers say they'd switch within a semester" is.

Step 5 · Factor in Time to Test

A simple Risk score identifies your biggest risks, but not necessarily the best one to test next. Add a third dimension: how quickly could you realistically get evidence?

Time-to-test scale (1 = very slow, 5 = immediate)

1: Only after significant development or a long timeframe.
2: Requires building substantial parts of the product.
3: A functional prototype or structured experiment.
4: Easy tests, landing pages, simple prototypes.
5: Immediately, through interviews, observation, or existing data.

Priority = Risk × Time-to-Test

This biases you toward high-risk assumptions that can also be tested quickly, dramatically improving your speed of learning.

The catch

Set a minimum importance threshold first.

A pure Risk × Testability score can let trivial-but-easy-to-test assumptions outscore ones that actually matter. Fix: exclude anything scoring below 4 on importance before you rank by priority.

Move 5

Design Your Experiments

An experiment is a procedure undertaken to make a discovery, test a hypothesis, or demonstrate a known fact. Here, you're using targeted experiments to validate or invalidate your top-priority assumptions, as cheaply and quickly as possible.

What Makes an Experiment Good

  • Measurable: a clear metric or observable outcome, known before you start.
  • Specific: it focuses on a single assumption, not several variables at once.
  • Time-bound: it produces results within a defined window.
  • Falsifiable: and the one most founders skip, the experiment must be able to realistically prove the assumption wrong, not just confirm it. A "no" result is exactly as valuable as a "yes."

Critique Before You Design

Experiment A

"We believe offering AI quizzes via a simple form will get 15 sign-ups in a week."

Tests real behavior, but doesn't measure whether the quizzes are actually useful. Stronger: also track completion and repeat requests. Interest isn't the same as value.

Experiment B

"We'll ask students if they'd pay €5/month, and see if 10 say yes."

Relies on stated intent, not real behavior. Stronger: ask for a pre-order or run a fake paywall. A real transaction is a far stronger signal.

Experiment C

"We'll build a full AI quiz generator and see if 70% of questions are rated useful."

Too resource-intensive for an early test. Stronger: manually generate quizzes with an existing AI tool first, test with 5–10 documents.

Two hypothesis formats

"We believe that [specific testable action] will result in [specific measurable outcome] within [timeframe]."
or
"If [we do X], by [taking Y action], we will [have this outcome], because [validation criteria] has been reached within [timeframe]."

Move 6 · Run, Review & Re-Prioritize

A plan is not evidence. Structure your remaining time into short cycles, alternating between launching and reflecting.

Launch Days

Confirm your setup is ready, and take the first concrete step to launch, send the form, post the ad, book the interviews.

Review Days

Share your results plainly. Interrogate the result. Update your experiment canvas. Decide your next experiment, and why.

Stick to your validation criteria

Decide what would change your mind, before you see the result.

It's tempting, once results come in, to quietly move the goalposts. Write your validation criteria down before you launch, and hold yourself to them afterward, even when the honest reading is uncomfortable.

After a full cycle of experimentation, resist the instinct to build a traditional pitch about market size and growth projections. What you've actually built is original insight, evidence-backed knowledge that gives you a genuine advantage, whether it points toward building exactly what you set out to build, or somewhere else entirely.

What Are You Actually Building?

Before you open a canvas in Week 1, you need to know which one to open. The simplest test: once you've built it, does the value live in something that exists independently of you, or does it only exist while a person is actively doing something for another person?

  • Packaged into a repeatable artifact a customer can use without you present each time? You're building a product.
  • Delivered live, through expertise or labor that must be re-performed for every customer? You're building a service.
  • Mostly social or environmental outcomes rather than revenue? You're building a social venture, whatever the delivery mechanism.

Quick Diagnostic

Answer these four questions and we'll suggest a canvas to start with.

1. Scalability: If you got 100× more customers tomorrow, would your costs grow...

2. Delivery: Can a customer receive the full value without ever speaking to a human on your team?

3. Ownership: After the transaction, does the customer...

4. Mission: Is your primary measure of success...

Most real ventures are hybrids, and that's fine. Pick the canvas that matches where most of your risk currently sits, and revisit the choice as your idea matures.

Glossary

Original Insight
Knowledge about a problem that gives you a genuine advantage in solving it, this workbook's version of “competitive advantage,” oriented toward early-stage founders and researchers.
Problem-Solution Fit
Evidence that your proposed solution actually addresses a real problem. Not a single milestone, a spectrum from reasoned to perceived to demonstrated to observed fit.
Assumption
A hypothesis about customers, problems, solutions, behaviors, markets, or economics that has not yet been validated with evidence.
Validation Criteria
The specific, concrete, ideally numeric threshold that would tell you an assumption is valid, invalid, or still unclear.
MVP (Minimum Viable Product)
The smallest version of your solution that lets you test your riskiest assumption, not a stripped-down version of your final vision.
Pivot
A structured change of direction in response to evidence, a change in problem, customer, solution, or business model, made deliberately rather than as a last resort.
Runway
How long your venture can operate before it runs out of money, typically expressed in months.
Desirability / Feasibility / Viability / Responsibility
The four lenses of business design: do people want it, can you build it, does it make business sense, and should it exist at all.
Jobs to Be Done
A framework holding that customers don't buy products, they “hire” them to make progress on a specific job in their life.
Smoke Test
An experiment that measures willingness to act, clicking, signing up, or paying, for a product that doesn't fully exist yet.
Dependency Logic
A way of reading your assumptions as a chain: market exists, problem is real, solution works, customers adopt, customers pay, economics work.
